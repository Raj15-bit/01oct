01

Money got dearer everywhere, and India paid first

Borrowing costs rose to levels not seen in over 20 years, foreign funds pulled money out of India, and Indian funds bought what they sold.

In short
  • The US government now pays more to borrow than at any time since 2002.
  • Foreign funds sold Indian shares day after day, and Indian funds bought almost all of it.
  • India's factories, tax collections and vehicle sales are strong, yet its share market fell for an 8th straight week.

Borrowing got dearer in every big economy

A yield is the yearly return a lender demands to lend to a government. On 1 October the US 10-year yield touched 5.34%, its highest since 2002.

Britain, Japan and France also paid more to borrow; the full bond story is in section 03.

So a dollar saver can now earn over 5% a year with almost no risk, which pulls money away from riskier markets like India.

How the chain runs to Indian companies

The RBI's rate committee meets from 5 October to 7 October. The repo rate (the rate at which the RBI lends to banks) is 5.25%, and Standard Chartered and others expect a rise of 0.25 percentage point to 5.50%.

Tata Motors already lists possible rate rises, next to diesel prices, as a risk for its truck business.

So strong demand may now meet dearer loans, which is why the RBI decision on 7 October matters.

02

The hard limits: diesel, wires, batteries and rain

Diesel, power lines, battery cells, AI chips, rail wheels and rain are all running below what India needs.

In short
  • Diesel is scarce around the world even though crude oil is flowing again.
  • India builds power lines at about half the speed its clean-energy plans need.
  • The weakest monsoon since 2015 has left India with its smallest sugar stock in a decade.

Diesel is the scarcest fuel

The diesel crack (the gap between the price of a barrel of diesel and a barrel of crude oil) rose to about $110. So refineries, not oil wells, are now the bottleneck, and countries are holding on to their diesel.

So countries that import diesel or depend on trucks pay first, and the refining shortage is expected to last through 2027.

Crude is flowing again, finished fuel is not

Gulf oil is moving through the Strait of Hormuz (the narrow sea passage out of the Gulf) again. Crude exports are near pre-war levels, but fuel exports are about half of normal, because the region's refineries were damaged.

  • Crude — Gulf oil exports at 23.3 million barrels a day.
  • Fuels — Gulf refined-fuel exports about 3 million barrels a day, against 4 to 6.5 million before the war.
  • Iraq — cut its price by up to $37 a barrel for cargoes loaded inside the Gulf, for war risk and freight.
  • Hormuz — 3 tankers were hit by unknown projectiles.
  • Sri Lanka — about 20 empty Iran-linked tankers drift off its coast, crews short of food and water.

One view is that the US has won the fight over Hormuz: crude flows are back, and Donald Trump says the US has "total control" of the strait. The opposite view is that the shortage only moved from crude to finished fuel, and ships are still being hit.

Battery cells: India assembles, China makes

India can put battery packs together, but buys most cells, the core unit inside each pack, from China.

  • PLI (production-linked incentive, a payment for each unit made in India) — the ₹18,100 crore scheme promised 50 GWh (gigawatt-hours) of cells; after 5 years about 1.4 GWh is built.
  • Imports — over 80% of cells are imported; India assembles about 23 GWh of packs a year.
  • Exide — switched on a 6 GWh cell plant in Bengaluru, using technology licensed from China's SVOLT.
  • Reliance — sought up to $1.1 billion of Chinese equipment for Jamnagar.
  • China's rules — export licences needed for cell-making machines since November 2025; since 15 September China can stop battery engineers from leaving.
  • Advait Energy — will buy up to 1 GWh of cells from China's Hithium for a Gujarat storage plant.
  • JSW Greentech — its first electric bus uses Chinese motors and controllers from CHTC Kinwin.

So the limit has moved from machines to people, which leaves India's battery and electric bus plans exposed to decisions made in Beijing.

Solar glass is short too

Each solar panel needs a sheet of special glass. India makes enough for about 17 GW to 18 GW of panels a year, against demand of over 50 GW, and imports the rest.

  • Borosil Renewables — 1,000 TPD (tonnes per day) running, 600 TPD more coming.
  • Vishakha Renewables — 660 TPD running, 1,260 TPD more due by FY27.
  • All makers — about 17 GW running and about 52 GW being built, including Waaree and Reliance plants, by CRISIL's count.

Rail wheels: the part that sets the pace

Indian Railways and private buyers have ordered many new wagons, but India makes too few forged wheels for them, so wagon makers now worry more about parts than orders.

So the pace of wagon deliveries over the next few quarters depends on how fast this plant passes railway tests.

03

The price of money: bonds, the rupee and the RBI

Lenders everywhere now want more to lend for long periods, and India pays through a weak rupee and rising local rates.

In short
  • America's long-term borrowing cost rose to its highest level since 2002.
  • US company debt and city bonds are falling fast, while US share prices stay calm.
  • The rupee is weak, Indian bond yields are rising, and the RBI is expected to raise rates.

America's long-term borrowing cost hits a 24-year high

A yield is what a lender demands to part with money for a set time. The yield on the US 10-year government bond rose above 5.3%, its highest since 2002.

It rose about 55 basis points in September (a basis point is one-hundredth of 1%), the worst September since 2022. The key US rates are below.

US 10-year5.30-5.34%Highest since 2002; up 138 bp from the March low
US 30-year5.64-5.66%Highest since 2002
US 2-year4.785%Fell 10 bp on 1 October
US 10-year real yield2.92%Yield after inflation; highest since 2008

The 2-year yield fell while the 10-year and 30-year rose. This is called a steeper curve (long-term rates rise faster than short-term rates), which means lenders worry more about inflation and government debt years ahead than about the next Fed meeting.

A softer inflation number, partly from a new method

The Fed's main price gauge, core PCE (prices households pay, leaving out food and fuel), rose 3.0% in August against 3.3% expected. Part of that drop came from a change in how 3 items are measured.

  • The method change — portfolio management fees, software and legal services are now counted differently. This alone cut core PCE by about 0.2 percentage points.
  • Hike odds — the chance of a Fed rate rise in October fell from about 70% to 34-37%.
  • Goldman Sachs — moved its call for the next Fed hike from October to December, and sees core PCE at 3.0% by year-end against the Fed's own 3.4%.
  • History — the Fed has never raised rates in an October just before a US election.
  • Neel Kashkari — the Minneapolis Fed president says price pressures are still high and still pencils in another hike.

So short-term rates eased on the hope of fewer hikes, but long-term rates kept rising, which means the bond market is not convinced inflation is beaten.

Bonds at 5.3%: worth buying or not?

Views split on whether US government bonds are now worth buying. The answer matters for India, because higher US yields make Indian shares look less attractive to foreign funds.

One view is that long-term rates have further to rise, because governments must keep borrowing more and buyers are few. The opposite view is that the current yield already pays enough, and that slower inflation will pull yields back down.

Oil, food and metals: who pays and who collects

Raw material prices rose across the board, and India's trade talks moved while the bills came in.

In short
  • Oil has 3 prices, and the price for real cargoes is far above the others.
  • Food, fuel and metal prices rose together, the most in years.
  • Reliance Industries, ONGC and farmers gain from new government decisions, while India's trade talks with the US stay unsettled.

Why oil is near $100 if America is "winning" Hormuz

Bloomberg columnist Javier Blas asked why oil sits near $100 if Donald Trump is winning at Hormuz. Crude flows have come back, but refined fuel has not (section 02).

  • Gulf exports — back to pre-war levels at 23.3m barrels a day; crude alone is 19m, or 108% of the 2025 average.
  • Iran — its oil exports through Hormuz are near zero.
  • Fujairah — the UAE port outside the strait now ships 3.6m barrels a day, up from 1.7m before the war.
  • Zero Hormuz — Abu Dhabi plans to make Fujairah a bypass through L'imad Holding, a $300bn fund, with BlackRock's GIP, Temasek and ADNOC.
  • Goldman Sachs — expects 60% of Gulf exports to be safe from Hormuz by end-2028, and Brent at $85 by year-end.
  • US output — a record 21.93m barrels a day in July (EIA).

Views split on where oil goes next. One view is that recovering Gulf flows and new bypass pipes will pull Brent well below $100 within months. The opposite view is that oil stays high because refined fuel is short and tankers are still being hit near Hormuz.

Copper, uranium and rare earths: supply is the problem

Freeport-McMoRan chief Kathleen Quirk says there is no deep list of ready-to-build copper mines waiting for higher prices. A new find takes about 2 decades to become a mine.

  • Copper — the price has moved from $4 to $5 to $6 a pound.
  • Uranium — the long-term contract price hit a record $96 a pound, above the 2007 peak.

The 5 stages of a shortage

When a product is scarce, its makers earn more on each sale for a while, until a rush to build factories marks the start of the end.

  • 1-2. Shock and squeeze — orders jump and factories run above 90% of capacity.
  • 3. Windfall — HFCL's operating margin went from 3.3% to 21.6% in 1 year; Steel Strips Wheels earned ₹314 per wheel against ₹262.
  • 4. Building rush — STL is spending ₹3,000 cr, HFCL ₹580 cr, Goodluck India ₹400-500 cr; Ather Energy is doubling capacity.
  • 5. Catch-up — new supply arrives and margins fall back.
